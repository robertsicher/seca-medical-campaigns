The Visible Progress Playbook

Five principles for helping patients understand change over time

Read the guide ↗
Clinical education · 14 September 2026 · Printable HTML resource

01

Establish a meaningful baseline

A baseline is most useful when the patient and clinician understand why it is being taken. Agree the question first. Record what is happening in the patient’s care and choose a limited set of parameters that will be meaningful at a future review.

Explain preparation and measurement in accessible language, check suitability and ask how the person prefers to receive information. Body composition should not become another unexplained test.

Conversation prompt: “What would you most like to understand about the changes you’re making?”

02

Explain more than bodyweight

Introduce the difference between total weight and body composition. Skeletal muscle, fat mass and other parameters describe different aspects of the result. They should not be reduced to a single judgement about the person’s health or effort.

Lean mass and skeletal muscle are not interchangeable terms. Likewise, a body composition reading cannot tell the whole story about strength, symptoms or daily function. Explain those boundaries as clearly as the result itself.

Conversation prompt: “The scales show the total. This result gives us some additional information to consider alongside your wider assessment.”

seca: understanding body composition results (PDF)

03

Make results understandable

Use the units and parameters that matter to this conversation. Avoid presenting every available graph at once. Describe an observation, explain the clinical context and check the patient’s understanding without testing or blaming them.

Consider the effects of language and visual labels. A result outside a reference range is not a diagnosis, and colour coding should not be used to imply certainty. Be alert to accessibility needs and the emotional impact of discussing weight and body composition.

Conversation prompt: “Would it help to look at one or two findings together, and talk about what they may mean?”

04

Review change over time

Compare measurements using consistent methods, preparation and units. Record relevant differences in illness, exercise or other circumstances. Unexpected changes should be reviewed rather than immediately treated as evidence that a programme has succeeded or failed.

seca Analytics 125 can display body composition changes, while myAnalytics can make results available to the patient when enabled by the provider. Decide how the team will explain those results and respond to questions.

Conversation prompt: “Here is what we measured at each review. Let’s consider what else was happening before we interpret the difference.”

seca UK: myAnalytics · seca UK: Analytics 125

05

Use the next measurement to support the next conversation

Close the review with an agreed purpose for any further measurement. The interval should be clinically relevant. Some patients may not need another measurement; for others, it may form one part of a wider review.

Offer clear information about accessing results and whom to contact. Provide a non-digital option when needed. Never imply that a patient must use an app to receive an appropriate explanation of their care.

Conversation prompt: “What would be useful to review next, and when would that information help us?”

06

Evaluate understanding, not just app use

A service can test whether patients understand why they were measured, can describe the main findings and know the next step. Ask about acceptability, burden and accessibility as well as satisfaction.

Measure repeat attendance or app use separately from clinical benefit. Neither demonstrates better outcomes or retention caused by the device. If those are objectives, use an evaluation design that can answer the question fairly.

  • Did the patient understand the purpose?
  • Was the information relevant and understandable?
  • Did they know how to ask questions?
  • Was the next step agreed?
