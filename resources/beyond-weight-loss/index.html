01

Why bodyweight tells only part of the story

A change in weight answers one question: has total body mass changed? It does not, by itself, describe the tissues or fluid contributing to that change. That distinction matters when a clinician and patient are reviewing a weight management programme.

Start with the purpose of the review. Are you trying to understand fat mass change, consider muscle quantity, explore an unexpected result or put a stable weight into context? The question should determine which additional information is useful. More measurements are not automatically better care.

02

Understanding BMI and its limitations

BMI relates bodyweight to height. It remains part of established assessment, but does not directly describe muscle quantity or distinguish the components of a person’s weight. Similar BMI values can therefore sit alongside different body composition.

“Beyond BMI” should mean additional context, not abandonment of BMI. NICE advises that bioimpedance should not substitute for BMI as a measure of general adiposity. Use body composition alongside the appropriate clinical assessment, not as a shortcut to diagnosis or treatment eligibility.

NICE NG246: overweight and obesity management · seca: understanding body composition results (PDF)

03

Understanding fat mass

Fat mass describes a component of body composition. A result expressed in kilograms and a result expressed as a percentage answer related but different questions. A percentage can change because its numerator, its denominator or both have changed.

When discussing a series of results, identify which measure is being compared and keep the unit consistent. Use relevant reference information without presenting an isolated reading as a complete statement about health.

seca: understanding body composition results (PDF)

04

Understanding skeletal muscle mass

Skeletal muscle mass concerns muscle tissue associated with movement and posture. It is not interchangeable with total lean mass or fat-free mass, which describe broader compartments. Avoid labelling every decrease in lean mass reported by a study as a loss of skeletal muscle.

Muscle quantity is also different from strength or physical function. Where those are clinical concerns, assess them appropriately rather than inferring them from a body composition result.

seca: understanding body composition results (PDF) · Cruz-Jentoft et al. (2019): EWGSOP2 consensus

05

Understanding visceral fat

Visceral fat is located within the abdomen around internal organs. It is distinct from the fat beneath the skin. A body composition estimate can provide an additional parameter for clinical review, but it is not a direct image of the abdomen.

Explain what the device reports and how the clinician will interpret it with the patient’s other information. Avoid diagnosing a condition or setting a treatment target from that value alone.

seca UK: Analytics 125

06

Why repeat measurement may be useful

A baseline creates a point of comparison. Subsequent measurements may help a clinical team describe trends that are not apparent from total weight. The value comes from an interpretable series linked to a relevant clinical question.

Consistency matters. Follow the instructions for use, aim for comparable preparation and timing, and document relevant context. Food, exercise and conditions affecting fluid balance can complicate interpretation. An unexpected result should trigger review of both the patient and measurement conditions.

Agree follow-up intervals on clinical grounds. This guide does not prescribe a universal schedule or a threshold at which treatment should change.

seca UK: myAnalytics

07

Body composition during GLP-1 treatment

In the STEP 1 exploratory DXA analysis, semaglutide treatment was associated with changes in fat mass and lean mass. The analysis provides a reason to distinguish components of weight change, while remembering that DXA lean mass is not a direct measure of skeletal muscle.

This study did not test whether introducing mBCA Alpha into a service improves treatment outcomes. It should not be used to promise muscle preservation, to predict an individual patient’s tissue changes or to prescribe changes to medication.

For a service considering BIA during GLP-1 treatment, define the measurement question, eligibility criteria and review protocol with the responsible clinical team.

Wilding et al. (2021): STEP 1 exploratory DXA analysis

08

Communicating change with patients

Begin with the patient’s question and ask permission to discuss weight or body composition. Explain two or three relevant findings in plain language. Distinguish the observed reading from your interpretation and acknowledge uncertainty.

A useful conversation can cover what changed, what else might explain it and what the clinical team wants to review next. Avoid “good” and “bad” body labels. Consider accessibility, emotional impact and whether a detailed numerical discussion is appropriate for the individual.

  • Confirm the patient understands why measurement was offered.
  • Explain the difference between weight, fat mass and skeletal muscle.
  • Use comparable units and a clear time interval.
  • Agree what the next clinical conversation will address.
09

Where mBCA Alpha fits

seca mBCA Alpha is an MDR-certified medical device using 8-point BIA. seca publishes a 24-second measurement period and identifies parameters including skeletal muscle, fat mass, visceral fat and phase angle. Analytics 125 supports review of results, while myAnalytics can provide patient access.

These features enable a measurement process; they do not establish a clinical outcome. Check patient suitability, staff training, digital requirements and the current documentation before introducing the device into a pathway. The full appointment includes more than the BIA measurement itself.

seca UK: mBCA Alpha · seca UK: Analytics 125 · seca UK: myAnalytics

10

From evidence to a clinical demonstration

Ask which reference methods support each parameter, which populations were studied and what limitations apply to your cohort. seca’s published validation information describes whole-body MRI for skeletal muscle mass and a four-compartment model for fat-free mass. Validation of a method is distinct from evidence of benefit in your service.

Bring a specific question to the demonstration: the cohort you see, the review you currently deliver and the additional information you hope to understand. If the fit is promising, agree a proportionate evaluation rather than assuming benefit.

seca UK: clinical validation · seca UK: clinical studies
